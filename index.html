    Los Angeles in 2026: the terrain

    Before choosing strategies, understand the ground. This section was updated in October 2026, and the figures that change fastest carry their dates and sources.

    🧭Use it in real life: quote dated figures with their source, and recheck them before a hearing or a vote. This platform operates on the homelands of the Tongva, Tataviam, Serrano, Kizh, and Chumash peoples.

    How many people

    The 2026 Greater Los Angeles Homeless Count, released on July 24, 2026, estimated 73,040 people experiencing homelessness across Los Angeles County, up 1.2 percent from 2025, and 45,194 people in the City of Los Angeles, up 3.4 percent. LAHSA said neither change was statistically significant, and totals remain below the 2023 and 2024 peaks.

    The number of people in families fell about 14 percent and the count of veterans fell from about 3,050 to about 2,260, while unsheltered homelessness rose, driven in part by more people living in RVs. The report linked the rise to living costs and to cuts in time-limited rental subsidies, which it said meant more than 5,000 fewer people moved into permanent housing. A point-in-time count is a one-night snapshot that likely misses people, so treat it as a floor.

    Why it happens here

    Decades of building too few homes for a growing region, single-family zoning across most residential land, rents that outpace wages, and a sprawling geography that puts services out of reach without a car. The California Housing Partnership estimates the county needs close to half a million more homes affordable to its lowest-income renters. Climate disasters add pressure: the January 2025 Eaton and Palisades fires destroyed more than 16,000 structures. In the 2026 count, 59 percent of people who had recently lost their housing named financial hardship as the main reason.

    Who is most affected

    Black Angelenos are dramatically overrepresented relative to their share of county residents, a legacy of redlining, discriminatory lending, and mass incarceration. Latino residents, especially recent immigrants and mixed-status families, face distinct barriers to shelter and benefits. Los Angeles has one of the largest urban American Indian and Alaska Native communities in the country, shaped by federal relocation policy, with elevated rates of homelessness. Veterans, young people leaving foster care, and people leaving jails and hospitals cycle through the system at high rates.

    Who is in charge now

    For three decades the Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority (LAHSA), a joint City–County agency created in 1993, coordinated the regional response. On April 1, 2025, the Board of Supervisors voted to create a County Department of Homeless Services and Housing (HSH). It launched on January 1, 2026, was scheduled to take over the County-funded programs LAHSA administered by July 1, 2026, and received $843 million from Measure A for 2026–27. LAHSA still runs the annual homeless count and the Winter Shelter Program, and City leaders opposed the split.

    Coordinating the City, the County, 87 other cities, and hundreds of providers is now the central governance challenge. The City's mayoral runoff on November 3, 2026 will shape what comes next.

    The legal ground

    In City of Grants Pass v. Johnson (June 2024), the U.S. Supreme Court held that enforcing camping bans does not violate the Eighth Amendment, even when no shelter is available. That widened what cities may do, but not what works: research and legal-aid reports document that sweeps without a housing offer destroy IDs and medications and break the case-management relationships that lead people indoors. California law bars landlords from refusing tenants because they pay with a housing voucher, and Stay Housed L.A. offers free legal help to tenants facing eviction.

    Where the money comes from

    Measure A (County, 2024)
    A half-cent sales tax, in effect since April 2025, that raises roughly $1 billion a year for homelessness services and affordable housing. It replaced Measure H.
    HHAP (State)
    Flexible Homeless Housing, Assistance and Prevention grants to cities, counties, and Continuums of Care. Each round's size and rules are set in the state budget.
    Proposition HHH (City, 2016)
    A $1.2 billion bond for supportive housing. City Controller audits flagged rising per-unit costs and long delays.
    Measure ULA (City, 2022)
    A tax on property sales above about $5 million that had raised about $1.4 billion by fall 2026, with spending lagging collection. A broader statewide initiative that would have capped such taxes was withdrawn in June 2026; its replacement, Proposition 43 on the November 3, 2026 ballot, would not apply to existing taxes like ULA.
    Homekey and Homekey+ (State)
    Funding to convert hotels, motels, and other buildings into housing. Homekey+ is funded by the 2024 Proposition 1 bond.
    Federal and Medi-Cal
    HUD Continuum of Care grants and housing vouchers, plus Medi-Cal CalAIM Community Supports that pay for housing navigation, deposits, and recuperative care.

    Sources: LAHSA 2026 Greater Los Angeles Homeless Count, as reported July 24, 2026; LA County Chief Executive Office HSH FAQ; LA County Board of Supervisors, April 1, 2025; CalMatters on Proposition 43, October 2026; City of Los Angeles Housing Department; California Housing Partnership.

    1,001 solution cards

    Nineteen flagship cards carry researched, Los Angeles–specific implementation notes and evidence. The other 982 are concept cards: starting points to scope, cost, and evidence-review before adoption. Cards that touch privacy, rights, clinical care, or land carry safeguard notes.

    🧭Use it in real life: save the cards you would bring to a meeting, then print them as a brief from your Toolkit.

    What do the labels mean?

    Effort (low, medium, or heavy lift) estimates how hard a strategy is to adopt. Timeline says when results could appear. For concept cards both are rough estimates.

    Evidence runs from strong (multiple rigorous evaluations) to emerging (early or descriptive). Concept means the idea has not been evidence-reviewed here.

    Safeguard notes appear when a strategy touches privacy (🔐), people's rights (🕊️), clinical care (🩺), or land and displacement (🪶). Open the card to read the safeguard.

              About this platform

              Research foundations

              Synthesizes research and public data from the Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority; the LA County Department of Homeless Services and Housing; the RAND Corporation; the California Policy Lab; the California Department of Housing and Community Development; the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development; the LA City Controller; legal-aid organizations including the Legal Aid Foundation of Los Angeles and Inner City Law Center; community organizations including LA CAN and United American Indian Involvement; and peer-reviewed literature from 2019 to 2026. Flagship cards summarize indicative evidence; concept cards are not reviewed. Verify against primary sources before citing in formal policy documents.

              Why a game?

              Games make it safe to practice hard things. Every mechanic here is tied to a real learning outcome: reading evidence, combining ideas, screening for harm, and naming the bottlenecks that block progress. The game never rewards speed or streaks, never exploits urgency, and never scores anyone for seeking help.

              How the collider works

              The collider crosses Los Angeles challenges with lenses, innovation modes, scales, deliverables, time horizons, and ideas borrowed from other fields, producing millions of distinct prompt chains. Resonance is a game signal that rewards good fit, novelty, and depth; it is not a measure of evidence. The synthesis engine reads only your own history on this device.

              Privacy and licence

              Zero Harm & Anti-Coercion

              This platform exists only to reduce suffering and expand housing dignity in Los Angeles. Nothing here may be used to surveil, profile, exclude, coerce, or criminalize people experiencing homelessness, or to target any individual. Strategies should be co-designed with people who have lived experience, offered on a voluntary basis, and judged by exits to safe, permanent homes. Bosses are broken systems, never people. No data is collected and nothing is tracked; your progress stays on this device until you erase it. If a use of this tool would increase harm, stop.

                  Need help today?

                  If anyone is in immediate danger, call 911. These free services cover Los Angeles County. Numbers were checked in October 2026; services change, so confirm details when you call.

                  • ☎️
                    Shelter, food, and services: 211 LA

                    Dial 2-1-1 any time, day or night, or visit 211la.org.

                  • 💬
                    988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline

                    Call or text 988, 24 hours a day.

                  • 🧠
                    LA County mental health and substance use help line

                    Call 1-800-854-7771, 24/7, in many languages. Press 1 for mental health, 2 for substance use, 3 for veterans and military families.

                  • 🏠
                    Facing eviction: Stay Housed L.A.

                    Free tenant legal help at 1-888-694-0040 or stayhousedla.org.

                  • 🛡️
                    LA County Domestic Violence Hotline

                    Call 1-800-978-3600 at any hour, or dial 2-1-1.

                  • 🎖️
                    Veterans: National Call Center for Homeless Veterans

                    Call 1-877-424-3838, 24/7, or visit va.gov.

                  • 🧭
                    Outreach for someone living outside: LA-HOP

                    The LA-HOP request form sends a homeless-services outreach team. It is for connecting people with help, never for complaints or enforcement. If you can, ask the person first and describe them respectfully.
